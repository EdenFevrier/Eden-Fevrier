The Smallest Units of Language Carry the Biggest Stories

Why I study the pieces inside words—and why Haitian Creole deserves to be studied on its own terms.

People often think that language lives in big things: speeches, books, debates, songs, and conversations. And it does. But some of the most revealing parts of language are almost invisible. They live inside words.

A prefix, a suffix, a small change in form, or a new combination of familiar elements can carry a surprising amount of information. These small pieces can tell us how speakers organize meaning, how they create new words, and how a language changes as its community changes.

"A word is never just a word. It is a record of how people have learned to make meaning together."

This is one reason I study morphology and morphosemantics: the relationship between the structure of words and the meanings they create. My work focuses especially on Haitian Creole and on derivational processes—ways speakers build new words from existing linguistic material.

Haitian Creole is often discussed through comparisons: compared with French, compared with other Caribbean creoles, compared with what outsiders expect a “language” to look like. Those comparisons can be useful, but they can also become limiting when they prevent us from taking Haitian Creole seriously on its own terms.

For me, studying Haitian Creole means paying attention to its internal logic. It means asking how speakers form words, how meaning is organized, and what the language itself can teach us about human creativity. It also means recognizing that no language becomes rich or expressive because an institution gives it permission. Languages are rich because their speakers use them to live, imagine, argue, pray, teach, joke, mourn, and build worlds together.

Why begin here?

I am building this website while I am still in the middle of the work. My research is developing. My writing is developing. My academic voice is developing too.

That is exactly why I wanted to begin now. Academic work can easily become invisible while we wait for the perfect publication, the complete dissertation, or the ideal moment to introduce ourselves. But research is not only the final document. It is also the questions that keep returning, the patterns that refuse to be ignored, and the long process of learning how to explain why they matter.

This blog will be a place for those questions. I will write about Haitian Creole, morphology, language learning, multilingualism, translation, research life, and the work of becoming a scholar in public.

I am starting with the smallest units of language because they remind me of something important: meaning does not always announce itself loudly. Sometimes it appears in a small form, attached to a familiar word, quietly changing what is possible.

That is where I want to begin.

More research notes and reflections are on the way.
