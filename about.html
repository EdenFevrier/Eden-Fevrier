About Me

Academic Background

I am a PhD student in Linguistics specializing in the morphosyntax and derivational morphology of Haitian Creole. My research examines how word-formation processes interact with grammatical structure, particularly how derivation can create new meanings, alter the syntactic behavior of words, and affect properties such as argument structure and transitivity.

A central part of my work focuses on productive derivational processes in Haitian Creole, including verbalizing suffixes such as -e, -en, and -te, as well as prefixation involving de- and dez-. I am interested not simply in identifying these forms, but in understanding what they reveal about the organization of the Haitian Creole lexicon and the relationship between morphology, syntax, and meaning.

My broader research interests include Haitian Creole morphosyntax, word formation, translation, information structure, Creolistics, and AI in Education. Across these areas, I am particularly interested in developing analyses that begin with the internal grammatical patterns of Haitian Creole.

This perspective is important to my work. Haitian Creole has a complex system with its own productive grammatical patterns. Studying those patterns on their own terms can contribute not only to a more accurate understanding of Haitian Creole, but also to broader questions in linguistic theory about how languages build words, encode meaning, and connect lexical structure with syntax.

Research and Teaching

My academic work extends beyond research. I teach Haitian Creole, French at the university level and am interested in language pedagogy, multilingualism, and the use of technology in language education. My teaching encourages students to examine language as a structured system while also understanding its cultural, social, and communicative dimensions.

I am also interested in the responsible use of artificial intelligence and computational tools in language learning and linguistic research. This includes exploring how emerging technologies can support language education, research workflows, and access to linguistic knowledge without replacing the analytical and human dimensions of scholarship.

Scholarship and academic community

I see scholarship as both an individual and collaborative endeavor. Conferences, research talks, academic organizations, teaching, and conversations with other scholars all contribute to the development of better questions and stronger analyses. I actively seek opportunities to present my work, engage with researchers in Creole studies and linguistics, and contribute to scholarly communities in which ideas can be tested, challenged, and refined.

An important part of my long-term work is helping strengthen the visibility of research on Haitian Creole. I want my scholarship to contribute to conversations within Creole studies while also demonstrating the relevance of Haitian Creole data to larger theoretical questions in morphology and syntax.

Professional Practice

Beyond academia, I am the founder of Garden of Languages and Services (GOLS), where I work across translation, interpretation, localization, linguistic consulting, and language education. This work allows me to apply linguistic knowledge to practical questions of communication across languages and contexts.

I also lead Linguistica Lab,a platform where I share ideas from linguistics, language learning, academia, and communication with a broader audience. The goal is to make serious scholarship accessible while preserving the depth and complexity of the ideas behind it.

To understand language seriously, we must examine not only what speakers say, but the grammatical systems that make those expressions possible.
